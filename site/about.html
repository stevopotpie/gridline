About Gridline

The bar was a forum

Gridline is built by Steven Sutcliffe, an analyst in Atlanta who spent twelve years behind a bar listening to football theories, and now puts them to an actual test.

Where the questions came from

For twelve years I tended bar in Georgia, most of it on game days with the games on behind me. I served tens of thousands of fans, and every Sunday the bar turned into a forum. People traded theories about what wins games and what covers spreads:

  • "Big underdogs always get the backdoor cover."
  • "They turned it over three times. They'll bounce back."
  • "That team never quits. They always rally late."

I had plenty of my own. What I didn't have was any way to find out which ones were true.

Putting them to a test

In 2024 I started learning data analysis, earned the IBM Data Analyst certificate, and learned to code. Gridline is where those bar questions finally get answered: public NFL play by play data back to 2015, every idea tested on seasons the model had never seen, and kept only if it earned its place.

Most ideas don't. Wind and rain hold up. Backdoor covers from garbage time, turnover bounce-backs and teams that "always" rally late do not. The ones that failed stay on the site with their numbers, because knowing what doesn't work is half the value.

What it is, and what it isn't

Gridline is a sandbox: a place to test a bet or an idea before you put money on it, see its chance to win, and see whether the price is worth it. It is not a picks service. It shows its record, including where it has no edge: the spread model has never beaten the 52.4% you need at -110, and Vegas is still more accurate on average.

How it's built

Python (pandas, scikit-learn, SciPy) on public nflverse data, live lines from about nine sportsbooks pulled several times a week, walk-forward backtests, and pages that rebuild on their own through GitHub Actions. Courtline, the NBA companion model, is built and tested the same way. All of the code is on GitHub.
